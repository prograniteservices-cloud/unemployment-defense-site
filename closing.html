Closing Argument

The employer had one job today: to prove misconduct. They have not done so.

Under South Carolina law, misconduct means a willful or deliberate disregard of the employer's interests. What the evidence in this hearing has shown is the exact opposite—a dedicated employee who was systematically denied the tools, the help, and the time needed to succeed, and who was then punished for the predictable result.

Let me summarize what the evidence has proven:

1. I was set up to fail—not negligent.
  • My helper was fired in August 2025. I asked for a replacement on August 13th: "Do we have any plans to hire me a helper?" None was provided.
  • I warned management on August 6th that rushing was creating quality risks: "We need adequate time to do quality control." Robbie acknowledged this: "I gotcha." Nothing changed.
  • On September 18th, overtime was banned. As the last person in the production line receiving unfinished work late in the day, overtime was my only tool to catch errors. That was taken away.
  • I requested a tape measure for two months. I was written up on December 4th for a measurement error. I was given a tape measure on December 5th—the day they fired me.

You cannot expect a worker to measure seams without a tape measure, or to lift 800 square feet of stone daily by hand without a helper or crane, and then call the inevitable errors "misconduct." These are textbook good faith errors caused by lack of tools—exactly what Regulation 47-101 protects.

2. The disciplinary actions were retaliation, not performance management.

I worked for Stonequarters for months with zero write-ups while pulling 50–60 hour weeks. Then, on November 13th, management asked about my plans to move. That very same day, I received my first write-up—for attendance that was well within the company's own 10-day allowance. Three weeks later, I was fired. That is not a coincidence. That is a paper trail manufactured to deny me benefits.

3. My departure proved it was never about me.

Andrew Bazil, the shop manager, confirmed in a text on February 11, 2026: "Nothing is leaving shop done right... Nobody knows shit. Blaming each other." The quality problems did not leave with me. They are systemic—caused by equipment failures, poor management, and a lack of resources. My text messages document machine malfunctions going back to November 2024, months before any write-up.

Furthermore, Robbie's own report to the unemployment office was not objective. Andrew confirmed: "He was yelling in his report... 'Barry's work is bad!!!'" That is personal bias, not evidence of misconduct.

In closing:

The employer has not shown a single instance where I willfully neglected my duties. Every issue they cited—measurements, quality, speed—was directly caused by tools I didn't have, help that was taken away, and time that was eliminated. South Carolina law is clear: discharge for inability, inefficiency, or good faith errors does not constitute misconduct.

I respectfully request that this determination be reversed and that my unemployment benefits be granted.

Thank you for your time and consideration.
